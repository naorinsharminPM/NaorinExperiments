Payments · RiskDecision window: < 100 ms

Catching fraud in milliseconds

Every time you tap a card, a model somewhere decides whether to approve the payment before the terminal finishes beeping. Fraud detection was one of the first jobs machine learning took on in finance, and it is still the clearest example of AI earning its keep.

Older systems relied on fixed rules: block anything over a limit, flag purchases from a new country. Rules are easy to explain, but fraudsters learn them quickly, and they annoy honest customers. Modern models look at hundreds of signals at once, such as device fingerprint, typing rhythm, merchant history and how this purchase compares with your usual week, and produce a single risk score.

The metric that matters

Teams obsess over the false-positive rate, not just fraud caught. Every legitimate card that gets declined is a customer who might switch banks. A good model catches more fraud and declines fewer real purchases.

The newer frontier is authorised push payment scams, where the real customer is tricked into sending money themselves. Here, models watch for behavioural signs of coercion, and the best response is often a well-timed question rather than a hard block.

Lending · UnderwritingInput: thin-file applicants

Rethinking credit decisions

Traditional credit scores work well for people with long borrowing histories and poorly for everyone else: young people, recent immigrants, gig workers. Lenders now use AI to read alternative data, such as rent payments, utility bills and cash-flow patterns from open banking, to judge whether someone can repay.

Done well, this widens access to credit. Done badly, it quietly rebuilds old biases, because historical data reflects who was lent to in the past. A postcode can stand in for ethnicity; a shopping pattern can stand in for gender.

  • Explainability is a legal requirement. Regulators expect lenders to give clear reasons for a decline, so black-box models need tools that show which factors drove each decision.
  • Fairness has to be tested, not assumed. Strong teams measure approval rates across groups before and after every model change.
  • Humans still own edge cases. Borderline applications go to an underwriter, with the model's reasoning attached.
Customer serviceChannel: voice · chat

The bank that picks up the phone

Most people still call their bank when something goes wrong: a lost card, a payment they don't recognise, a mortgage question. For years, those calls hit a phone menu that asked you to "press 1" and then put you on hold anyway.

Conversational AI is changing that. Voice agents built on large language models can understand people speaking naturally, verify who they are, and complete real tasks: freezing a card, explaining a charge, rescheduling a payment. When the problem is complex or emotional, they hand over to a person with the full context, so the customer doesn't have to repeat themselves.

Why finance is a hard test

A voice agent in a bank has to follow strict scripts for disclosures, never invent a balance or an interest rate, and resist social-engineering attempts. Guardrails and grounding in real account data matter more here than in almost any other industry.

The goal isn't to remove people from service. It is to let them spend their time on the conversations that actually need judgement and empathy.

RegTech · AML / KYCOutput: suspicious activity reports

Compliance at machine speed

Banks spend enormous sums on anti-money-laundering (AML) and know-your-customer (KYC) checks. Much of that effort goes to reviewing alerts that turn out to be nothing. Rule-based monitoring systems are tuned to be cautious, so analysts wade through thousands of false alarms to find the few that matter.

AI helps at three points in the process:

  • Onboarding. Document checks and face matching verify identity in minutes instead of days.
  • Monitoring. Network analysis spots rings of linked accounts that no single-transaction rule would catch.
  • Investigation. Language models draft case summaries and the narrative for a SAR, which an analyst then reviews and signs off.

The analyst stays accountable. What changes is that their time goes into judgement rather than copy-pasting between five different systems.

Wealth · MarketsAudience: retail investors

Advice for everyone

Personal financial advice used to be something only wealthy clients got. Robo-advisers changed that a decade ago by automating portfolio building and rebalancing. The next wave goes further: assistants that can look at your whole financial picture and explain, in plain language, what a decision means for you.

On the institutional side, AI reads earnings calls, filings and news faster than any analyst team, and quantitative funds have used machine learning in trading strategies for years. The edge from any single signal fades quickly once everyone has it, so the advantage shifts to data quality and speed of iteration.

The open question

Who is responsible when an AI gives bad financial guidance? Rules on suitability and duty of care were written for human advisers. Expect regulators to keep clarifying how they apply to automated advice.

What ties these together

In each of these areas, the winners aren't the companies with the cleverest model. They are the ones that pair AI with clear accountability: good data, honest measurement of errors, an easy path to a human, and the ability to explain a decision to a customer or a regulator. Finance runs on trust, and AI in fintech works when it adds to that trust.
